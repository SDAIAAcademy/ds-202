Days 1 and 2 dealt with what is in the files. Today is about what is not: values that are blank, rows that never arrived, and values that are recorded and wrong. No new tool is needed, only a household table and arithmetic. Every lab starts from the supplied table data/reference/household_q3.csv.gz, so yesterday's results are not required. The first question

Ask why a value is missing before deciding what to do about it

  • Household size is blank for 18.1% of the 35,665 households of the question
  • Without a size, a household cannot be classified as eligible or not
  • Dropping them, filling them, or reporting a range: each is right under one reason for the blank and wrong under another

Where reasons come from

From the people who produced the data, the source note, and data from outside. Rarely from the column itself.

6,461 households have no recorded size. The catalog entry speaks of 18% of household sizes missing: 18.0% of all 39,820 households, 18.1% of the target population. Ask the room for reasons a size might be blank before showing the three mechanisms; they usually produce all three in their own words.

Three mechanisms

A blank can depend on nothing, on what you see, or on the value itself

MechanismThe blank depends onDropping the rows
MCAR · completely at randomnothingloses precision, adds no bias
MAR · at random given what is recordedfields you can see: a branch, a datebiased, unless you work within those fields
MNAR · not at randomthe missing value itselfbiased, and the recorded data cannot show by how much

The names are Rubin's (1976). They describe the relation between a blank and the data, not a property of a column: one column can hold all three.

Keep the definitions short and use the case for everything else. The third column is what matters for an analyst. Sources: Rubin, Inference and missing data, Biometrika 1976; van Buuren, Flexible Imputation of Missing Data, chapter 1.

There is no neutral option

Every way of handling a blank is an assumption about the households behind it

Complete cases

Drop the rows. Assumes the dropped households are like the kept ones.

One value for all

Fill with the median. Assumes every blank household is typical.

A value from similar households

Fill from a group. Assumes that within the group, blank and recorded households are alike.

Lab 3.2 computes the headline all three ways, on the same 35,665 households.

"I did not impute, I only dropped the rows" is the sentence to defuse. Dropping is the strongest assumption of the three here, because Lab 3.1 showed the dropped households are larger. Sources: van Buuren, Flexible Imputation of Missing Data, sections on listwise deletion and single imputation.

Lab 3.2, step 1

Complete cases: 1.985, from the households that were easy to record

  • 29,204 households take part; 6,461 do not
  • The 6,461 are larger, more often eligible, and receive 2 or 3 parcels per visit
  • Remove them, and the average of the eligible households that remain is too low
  • 1.985 is below the standard of 2: the network "fails"
What goes wrong. The code runs, no value was changed, and the result is reported as "the data". The assumption was never written down.

The estimate: among households with a known size and income per member at most 1,000 SAR, parcels in the quarter divided by households, divided by three months. Most libraries drop rows with missing values silently in a group-by or a model; complete-case analysis is the default nobody chose.

Change the question

"Which estimate is right?" cannot be answered. "How far can it move?" can

1 · Logical boundsassume nothing about the blank households
→
2 · A plausible rangeassumptions you can defend, stated
→
3 · A recommendationone estimate, its range, its reason
A sensitivity analysis varies what you assumed and reports what happens to the conclusion. It does not make the missing data less missing.

The range of this session comes from assumptions, not from sampling: it would not shrink with more households, only with more knowledge about the blank ones. Intervals for sampling variation are DS-201. Sources: van Buuren, Flexible Imputation of Missing Data, on sensitivity analysis.

Lab 3.3, step 1

Whatever the 6,461 households are, the estimate lies between 1.775 and 2.133

  • Each blank household is eligible or not; you do not know which
  • Lowest: exactly those that collected few parcels are eligible
  • Highest: exactly those that collected many are eligible
  • No assumption about sizes can leave this interval

Honest, and too wide

The bounds straddle the standard of 2. They take "every blank household that collected nothing is eligible" as seriously as anything else.

The recipe in the lab: sort the blank households by parcels, add them to the eligible group one at a time, track the running average, keep the minimum (and, sorted the other way, the maximum). These are worst-case bounds in the spirit of partial identification. Their use is to show the reader what the missing field could do at worst, before any assumption is spent.

The other half of data quality

A value can be present, valid, and wrong

  • Household size is reported by the household at registration and is not verified
  • It passes every rule: an integer between 1 and 20
  • No validation suite can see that 7 should have been 5
  • Only a second measurement can: here, 289 audited households with a recorded size

Two kinds of error

Random: up as often as down, for anybody.
Systematic: in one direction, or for one kind of household.

Tomorrow's rules test validity; today's point is that validity is not accuracy. Self-reported quantities with a consequence attached (eligibility, a benefit, a fee) are where systematic error is most likely.

Proxies

A proxy measures what it measures, not what you wish it measured

  • Parcels per handout are set by the desk from the family card: a proxy for the verified size band
  • It disagrees with the recorded size for 1.5% of households that collected: a list for review
  • It is silent about households that never collected
  • 323 households are eligible by the register and never collected in nine months
What goes wrong. Defining "eligible" as "collected regularly". Every eligible household that was not served leaves the denominator, and every district looks well served.

The 323 are two groups the proxy cannot separate: households that over-reported and were turned away at the desk, and under-served households, who are the reason for the whole analysis. Use a proxy to flag records for review; do not let it redefine the question.

Which way does the headline move?
